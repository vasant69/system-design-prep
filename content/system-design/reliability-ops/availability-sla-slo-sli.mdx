<SimpleDefinition>

An SLI (Service Level Indicator) is a directly measured metric — for example, actual uptime percentage over the last 30 days. An SLO (Service Level Objective) is an internal target set for that metric, such as 99.9% uptime. An SLA (Service Level Agreement) is an external, often contractual, commitment made to customers, usually backed by financial penalties if missed. Availability is the most common example of a metric used as both an SLI and an SLO.

</SimpleDefinition>

## Hinglish Deep Explanation

<Hinglish>

Yeh teen letters — SLI, SLO, SLA — interview mein aksar ek hi cheez samajh li jaati hain, lekin yeh actually ek **pipeline** hain, ek dusre pe based. Confuse karna easy hai isliye clear definitions se shuru karte hain, phir asli meat — "nines" ka actual math aur error budget — pe aayenge.

### Teeno ka relationship

- **SLI (Indicator)** — yeh sirf ek **measurement** hai. Raw number. "Pichle 30 din mein humara service 99.95% time up tha." Koi target nahi, sirf fact.
- **SLO (Objective)** — yeh ek **internal target** hai jo team apne liye set karti hai us SLI pe. "Humara goal hai 99.9% uptime maintain karna." Yeh ek promise hai jo tum khud se karte ho, engineering culture ke liye.
- **SLA (Agreement)** — yeh ek **external, often contractual** commitment hai customer ko. "Agar humara uptime 99.9% se neeche gaya kisi mahine mein, tumhe bill pe credit milega." Yahan paisa involved hota hai — legal/financial consequence.

Simple flow: pehle tum kuch **measure** karte ho (SLI), phir uske basis pe ek **internal goal** set karte ho (SLO), aur phir agar zaroorat pade to customer ko ek **weaker, safer version** of that goal contractually promise karte ho (SLA). Order yehi hota hai — SLI se SLO nikalta hai, SLO se SLA nikalta hai, ulta nahi.

### "Nines" ka asli math

Availability percentages abstract lagte hain jab tak tum unhe actual downtime mein convert nahi karte. Ek saal mein 525,600 minutes hote hain (365 din). Yahan exact math hai:

- **99% ("two nines")** — 1% downtime allowed = **3.65 din/saal** = 87.6 hours/saal down reh sakta hai. Yeh production ke liye bahut loose hai — matlab har mahine roughly 7 hours ka outage "acceptable" hai.
- **99.9% ("three nines")** — 0.1% downtime = **8.76 hours/saal**. Roughly 43 minutes/mahina.
- **99.99% ("four nines")** — 0.01% downtime = **52.6 minutes/saal**. Roughly 4.3 minutes/mahina.
- **99.999% ("five nines")** — 0.001% downtime = **5.26 minutes/saal**. Roughly 26 seconds/mahina.

Har additional nine downtime ko roughly **10x kam** kar deta hai. Yeh linear improvement nahi hai — yeh **exponential** hai, aur isiliye cost curve bhi exponential hoti hai.

### Kyun har additional nine itna mehenga hota hai

Yahan wahi part hai jo interview mein depth dikhata hai. 99% se 99.9% jaana — matlab downtime 3.65 din se ghatakar 8.76 hours karna — mostly "basic hygiene" se ho jaata hai: proper monitoring, restart-on-crash, thoda sa redundancy.

Lekin 99.9% se 99.99% jaana — matlab 8.76 hours se 52.6 minutes — bahut zyada engineering maangta hai:

- **Multi-region deployment** — ek single region/datacenter down hone se poora service down nahi hona chahiye. Isse infra cost roughly double ya usse zyada ho jaata hai.
- **Automated failover** — manual intervention (koi engineer 2am pe uthke server restart kare) is level ki availability ke liye kaafi slow hai. Failover automated hona chahiye, seconds mein detect aur react kare.
- **Zero-downtime deploys** — agar deploy karne ke liye service ko restart karna padta hai with even 30 seconds downtime, aur tum din mein multiple baar deploy karte ho, budget khatam ho jaayega sirf deploys se hi. Rolling deploys, blue-green deployment, canary releases zaroori ban jaate hain.
- **Extensive on-call aur incident response tooling** — jab kuch fail ho, response time seconds mein honi chahiye, minutes mein nahi.

99.99% se 99.999% jaana aur bhi harder hai — ab tumhe individual dependency-level redundancy, chaos engineering (proactively failures inject karke test karna ki system resilient hai), aur bahut mature operational discipline chahiye. Yeh cost curve **linear nahi, exponential** hai — har extra nine ka cost pichle nine se kaafi zyada hota hai, jabki incremental benefit (downtime minutes mein reduction) shrink hota jaata hai. Isliye har system ko five-nines chahiye hi nahi — ek internal admin tool ke liye 99.9% kaafi hai, ek payment gateway ke liye 99.99%+ zaroori ho sakta hai.

### SLO SLA se strict kyun hona chahiye

Yeh ek genuinely important, thoda subtle point hai: **SLO hamesha SLA se strict (tighter) hona chahiye**, kabhi loose nahi. Kyun?

Socho tumhara customer-facing SLA hai 99.9% uptime (penalty ke saath agar miss ho). Agar tumhara internal SLO **bhi** 99.9% hai — same number — to koi buffer nahi hai. Ek chhota sa unexpected incident, aur tum seedha SLA breach kar dete ho, penalty pay karte ho, customer trust lose karte ho.

Isliye smart teams apna SLO **tighter** rakhte hain — jaise SLO = 99.95%, jabki SLA = 99.9%. Yeh 0.05% ka gap ek **buffer** hai. Agar internal SLO thoda miss bhi ho jaaye (jo realistically hota rehta hai), tumhare paas abhi bhi room hai SLA ke andar rehne ka. SLO ek "early warning system" ki tarah kaam karta hai — jab SLO breach hone lagta hai, team ko pata chal jaata hai ki kuch fix karna hai, **before** it becomes a customer-facing, contractual, financial problem.

### Error budget — ek genuinely useful mental model

Agar tumhara SLO 99.9% hai, iska matlab hai tumhare paas **0.1% ka explicit "budget"** hai failure ke liye. Yeh 0.1% — yeh koi accident nahi hai jo avoid karna hai, yeh ek **spend-able resource** hai.

Yeh mindset shift bahut important hai. Iska matlab yeh nahi hai ki "zero downtime chahiye hamesha" — woh unrealistic aur counter-productive goal hai (aur jaisa upar dekha, cost bhi exponentially badhta hai). Iska matlab hai: **tumhare paas ek defined amount of allowed failure hai, aur tumhe decide karna hai usse kaise spend karna hai.**

Mature teams is budget ko **shipping fast** ke liye use karte hain — naye features risky deploy karna, experiments run karna, aggressive scaling changes try karna — kyunki unke paas budget hai absorb karne ke liye chhoti mistakes. Agar budget already khatam ho chuka hai (bahut saare recent incidents ho chuke hain), to team automatically **zyada conservative** ho jaati hai — naye risky deploys freeze kar dete hain, sirf reliability-fixing work pe focus karte hain jab tak budget replenish na ho jaaye.

Yeh velocity aur stability ke beech ek **explicit, data-driven balance** hai — na sirf "ship fast, break things" (jo customer trust todta hai), na sirf "never break anything" (jo velocity ko zero kar deta hai aur unnecessary cost create karta hai chasing nines jo business ko chahiye hi nahi thi).

</Hinglish>

<Callout type="tip">

Interview mein error budget mention karna ek strong signal hai. Line jo kaam karti hai: "SLO chahe humein 99.9% availability maintain karni ho, iska matlab hai humare paas 0.1% ka error budget hai — hum usse conservatively bachaate nahi, hum usse strategically spend karte hain fast shipping ke liye, jab tak SLA breach na ho."

</Callout>

## Real World Example

<RealWorld company="Google">

Google ne hi SRE (Site Reliability Engineering) practice ke through error budget concept ko mainstream kiya. Unka rule simple hai: agar ek service apna error budget kisi quarter mein use kar chuka hai, naye feature launches **freeze** ho jaate hain us service ke liye jab tak reliability wapas track pe na aa jaaye — yeh ek organizational-level enforcement hai, sirf ek suggestion nahi.

</RealWorld>

<RealWorld company="Amazon">

AWS apne services (S3, EC2, etc.) ke liye publicly published SLAs deta hai, jaise S3 Standard ke liye 99.9% monthly uptime commitment, penalty ke saath service credits ke form mein agar miss ho. Lekin AWS ka internal SLO in numbers se kaafi tighter hota hai — unki actual measured availability historically SLA se better rahi hai, exactly is buffer ki wajah se.

</RealWorld>

<RealWorld company="Flipkart">

Flipkart jaisi e-commerce company ke liye Big Billion Days jaisa sale event ek "error budget stress test" hota hai — normal mahine mein jo budget dheere dheere spend hota, sale ke ek din mein woh kaafi tezi se consume ho sakta hai agar checkout ya payment flow mein koi issue aaye. Isliye aise events se pehle teams deliberately conservative ho jaati hain — risky deploys freeze kar dete hain event se kuch din pehle, taaki poora budget event day ke liye reserved rahe.

</RealWorld>

## Numbers That Matter

<Numbers>

- 99% uptime = 3.65 days/year downtime allowed (~87.6 hours)
- 99.9% uptime = 8.76 hours/year downtime allowed (~43 minutes/month)
- 99.99% uptime = 52.6 minutes/year downtime allowed (~4.3 minutes/month)
- 99.999% uptime = 5.26 minutes/year downtime allowed (~26 seconds/month)
- Typical gap between internal SLO and external SLA — often 0.05% to 0.1 percentage points as buffer
- A service with a 99.9% SLO effectively has a 0.1% error budget — roughly 43 minutes of allowed badness per month to spend deliberately

</Numbers>

## Interview Angle

<InterviewAngle>

Common prompt: **"How would you define reliability targets for this system?"** ya "what's the difference between SLA, SLO, and SLI?" Interviewer testing kar raha hai ki tumhe pata hai yeh sirf buzzwords nahi hain, balki ek design decision hai jo trade-offs involve karti hai.

Model answer shape: Define teeno cleanly (measurement to internal-target to external-contract), phir turant explain karo ki SLO SLA se strict kyun hona chahiye (buffer for breach protection), aur error budget concept laao unprompted — "agar SLO 99.9% hai, humare paas 0.1% ka budget hai jo hum strategically spend karte hain fast iteration ke liye, na ki sirf avoid karte hain." Extra depth ke liye add karo ki different components alag SLOs le sakte hain — jaise payment flow ko 99.99% chahiye lekin ek internal analytics dashboard ko 99.5% kaafi hai, aur higher nines exponentially costly hote hain (multi-region, automated failover, zero-downtime deploys) isliye har cheez ko five-nines chase karna waste hai.

</InterviewAngle>

## Common Mistakes

<Mistake>

SLA aur SLO ko same cheez samajh lena, ya unhe same number pe set kar dena — isse koi buffer nahi bachta aur ek chhota internal miss seedha customer-facing contractual breach ban jaata hai.

</Mistake>

<Mistake>

"Hume 99.999% availability chahiye" bol dena bina yeh soche ki har additional nine ka cost exponentially badhta hai — multi-region, automated failover, zero-downtime deploys sab chahiye hote hain, aur agar business ko itni reliability chahiye hi nahi (jaise ek internal tool), yeh pure waste hai.

</Mistake>

<Mistake>

Error budget ko "avoid karne wali cheez" samajhna instead of "spend karne wala resource" — is mindset se team overly conservative ho jaati hai aur unnecessarily slow ship karti hai jabki unke paas actually room tha risk lene ka.

</Mistake>

<Mistake>

Poore system ke liye ek hi SLO set kar dena, jabki alag-alag components ki reliability requirement kaafi alag hoti hai — jaise checkout flow aur ek "recommended for you" widget ko same 99.99% target dena, jo widget ke liye overkill aur wasteful hai.

</Mistake>

<Mistake>

Downtime numbers ko percentage mein hi discuss karna bina actual minutes/hours mein convert kiye — "99.9%" abstract lagta hai, "8.76 hours saal mein" concrete lagta hai aur decision-making ke liye zyada useful hai.

</Mistake>

## Quick Revision

<QuickRevision>

- SLI = measured metric (fact); SLO = internal target (goal); SLA = external contract (promise + penalty)
- Order matters: SLI informs SLO, SLO informs SLA — not the other way around
- Downtime math: 99% = 3.65 days/year, 99.9% = 8.76 hours/year, 99.99% = 52.6 min/year, 99.999% = 5.26 min/year
- Each extra nine cuts downtime ~10x but costs exponentially more (multi-region, automated failover, zero-downtime deploys)
- SLO should always be stricter than SLA — the gap is your buffer against contractual breach
- Error budget = allowed failure amount from your SLO, treated as a resource to spend, not just avoid
- Spend the error budget on shipping fast; when it's exhausted, freeze risky changes and stabilize
- Not every component needs five-nines — match the target to what the business actually needs

</QuickRevision>
